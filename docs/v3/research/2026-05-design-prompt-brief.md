# 9mrentals — Claude Design Brief
> Hand this to Claude Design as-is. It is a brief, not a spec.
> Last updated: May 2026

---

## What 9mrentals is

An action camera rental service in Kuala Lumpur. GoPro, Insta360, DJI — rented by the day, collected in person, returned after the adventure.

But the product is not the point. The product is the means.

The real thing 9mrentals sells is **permission**. Permission to document a moment properly without spending RM 3,000 to own a camera you'll use three times a year. Permission to push the gear hard without the fear of a scratch costing you. Permission to go on the trip and come back with footage worth watching in ten years.

The brand's core question — the one that should haunt every page — is: **"If not now, when?"**

---

## Who this is for

Young adults, mid-20s to early 30s, Klang Valley. Tech-savvy. They have disposable income but they're not stupid with it. They go on adventures — diving in Tioman, hiking Gunung Ledang, road trips, travel vlogs. They want to document these moments properly. They are not gear nerds. They just don't want to look back at a life-defining trip and have nothing but shaky phone footage.

They've tried to rent gear before. It was a nightmare — WhatsApp catalogs, Google Forms, 50% deposits to strangers, no real-time availability, no trust. The bar is embarrassingly low. 9mrentals clears it easily just by being a real, modern platform.

## The feeling this site needs to create

### Dark. Weighted. Cinematic.

Not dark like a hacker tool. Dark like a film poster. Like standing at the edge of something before you jump. The darkness is intentional — it creates atmosphere, it makes the gear imagery pop, it signals that this is a brand that takes the adventure seriously.

### Premium, but not luxury.

This is not a Rolex ad. It's not aspirational in a distant, unattainable way. It's premium in the way a well-made piece of kit feels premium — solid, purposeful, no wasted material. The user should feel like they're dealing with people who know what they're doing. Not a startup trying to look expensive. A brand that's earned its confidence.

### Quiet confidence.

The site should never shout. No exclamation marks. No "AMAZING DEALS." No countdown timers. The copy is short, declarative, and lands like a punch. The design gives it room to breathe. White space is not emptiness — it's weight. Every element earns its place.

### Identity-first.

The brand doesn't describe its audience — it *is* its audience. The site should feel like it was built by someone who has been on the other side of a bad rental experience. Not a company serving adventurers — adventurers serving adventurers. The gear is not the hero. The moment the gear enables is the hero.

### Craft as the standard.

Obsessive precision in spacing, type, and motion. Deep simplicity — the page should feel effortless because enormous care went into it, not because it's sparse. Typography as atmosphere: the way type is set creates feeling before the words are read. Restraint creates tension. Emptiness does work. Opinionated design builds trust — a site with a clear point of view feels more credible than one trying to please everyone.

### Red. Used with intention.

Red is the brand accent. It's a signal, not decoration — when it appears, it should mean something. The specific shade is yours to decide: sharp and saturated, not warm or muted.

### Motion: intentional, polished, atmospheric.

Every animation earns its place. If it doesn't serve the atmosphere or orient the user, it doesn't exist.

The energy lives in the design — typography, darkness, the red accent — not in how things move. Motion's job is to be fluid and invisible in the background, and precise and considered where it matters.

Where it matters: depth and parallax that make the page feel like a window into a world that extends beyond the frame. Interactions that respond, not just acknowledge — the interface should feel alive to the user's presence, not merely reactive to their clicks.

## The emotional journey

The page is not a brochure. It's a story — and it's also a conversion funnel, just described in human terms. Every design decision — structure, hierarchy, pacing, white space — should move the user through these stages in order:

**Desire → Relevance → Permission → Confidence → Validation → Resolution → Identity**

Two things to understand about this arc:

**Desire is for the moment, not the gear.** The user should want the dive, the summit, the road trip — and feel the gear as the thing that makes it permanent. Never lead with the camera. Lead with what the camera captures.

**Identity is the destination, not the close.** The final beat is not a call to action. It's a mirror. The user should recognise themselves in it — someone who does things others only talk about. This is the hardest stage to design and the most underused. It should feel like a film's last frame: the one that stays with you after the credits roll.

The structure is yours to decide. These are the emotional destinations, not the sections.

## Voice and copy direction

Short. Declarative. Second person. Present tense.

The register to match — these three lines are load-bearing. They define the brand's voice. Everything else should sound like it belongs in the same sentence as one of these:

> "All the gear. Zero ownership."
> "Use it fully. That's what it's for."
> "If not now, when?"

**Never:** exclamation marks, "seamless," "hassle-free," "world-class," "premium," passive voice, corporate we, hedging ("we try to..."), explaining the joke.

Every section needs one line that could stand alone on a billboard. Everything else supports it.

**The brand's four values — in order, as a cycle:**
Seek Discomfort → Own It → Be Real → Full Send → (back to the beginning)

These aren't taglines. They're the character of the brand. The copy should feel like someone who lives by these values wrote it.

## What to design

This is a homepage for an e-commerce rental service. A visitor arrives, gets convinced, and needs to be able to browse and book gear before they leave. Those are the two jobs: make them want it, make it easy to get it.

**Primary action:** browsing and booking a kit.

**Success criterion:** a visitor who has never heard of 9mrentals should leave either having booked, or knowing exactly why they would next time.

Before generating any designs, propose 4 distinct visual directions for this homepage. For each, produce a mood board-style page: a colour palette, a headline set in the proposed typeface, and 2–3 sentences describing the atmosphere and character. Wait for a selection before proceeding.

**You are not constrained by any existing implementation.** Propose whatever structure best serves the emotional arc and the product purpose. Cut sections, combine them, break convention where it creates a stronger moment.

Choose your own colour palette. Choose your own typography. The directional constraints: near-black backgrounds, red as the single brand accent. The palette should feel like it belongs to this brand, not borrowed from another.

The goal is not to execute a spec. The goal is to create a homepage that makes someone feel something — and then book a kit.

## What not to do

- Generic SaaS dark mode: purple gradients, glowing cards, "AI aesthetic"
- Luxury brand aloofness — this is accessible, not exclusive
- Outdoor gear brand ruggedness (REI, Patagonia) — too earthy, wrong register
- Startup landing page energy — social proof counters, "Join 10,000+ users," feature grids
- RGB maximalism — the dark aesthetic without the discipline
- Explaining the brand — show it, don't describe it
- Hedging copy — either you do it or you don't
- Decorative motion — every animation must serve the atmosphere or the user, not itself
